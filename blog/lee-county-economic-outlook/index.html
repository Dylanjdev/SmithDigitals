The economic outlook of Lee County

Is a turnaround possible?

Population decline is real. So are the signals moving in the other direction: migration, tourism, investment, entrepreneurship, and a renewed push to connect the county's businesses.

The short version

A turnaround is possible—but it would look like momentum, not a miracle.

Lee County does not need to become a major city. It needs more of its existing assets working together: local businesses, visitors, industrial land, housing, infrastructure, digital visibility, and the people already committed to the community.

If You Only Look at the Population, You Might Miss the Bigger Story

If you only looked at Lee County's population numbers, it would be pretty easy to come away with a pessimistic view of the future.

And honestly, some of that pessimism is justified.

Lee County has lost a significant number of residents over the past 15 years. The county had 25,587 people in 2010, compared with 22,173 in 2020 and an estimated 21,642 in 2025. That's a loss of nearly 4,000 people since 2010. Source: U.S. Census Bureau

Those aren't numbers we should ignore or try to explain away.

But population isn't the entire economy.

There are other numbers that deserve just as much attention: people moving into the county, hundreds of thousands of visitors coming to Cumberland Gap, millions of dollars being invested in infrastructure, existing businesses expanding, industrial land available for development, and a large number of small businesses already operating in the county.

None of that guarantees that Lee County is about to experience a boom.

I don't think it would be honest to say that.

But I do think it raises a much more interesting question:

What happens if Lee County actually starts connecting all of these pieces?

That is what this article is really about.

Not pretending Lee County has already turned things around.

Not pretending population decline doesn't matter.

And not claiming that one new business or one grant is going to fix everything.

Instead, this is an attempt to look at where Lee County actually stands, what the numbers are telling us, where tourism fits into the picture, and what could realistically happen if the county begins building on the assets it already has.

Where Lee County Actually Stands

Let's start with the uncomfortable part.

Lee County is smaller than it used to be.

The Census Bureau estimates the county had 21,642 residents in 2025, down 2.4% from its 2020 population estimate base of 22,173. The 2010 Census counted 25,587 residents. Source: U.S. Census Bureau

That long-term decline has real consequences.

When a county loses people, it doesn't just lose a number on a spreadsheet.

It can lose customers.

It can lose workers.

It can lose entrepreneurs.

It can lose students.

It can lose families who would have eventually started businesses, bought homes, or invested in the community.

And when fewer people are buying things locally, it becomes harder for some businesses to survive.

That can create a cycle:

Fewer people → fewer customers → fewer businesses → fewer jobs → more people leave.

That's the cycle Lee County has to fight.

But there's another side to that equation.

If the county can bring money and people in from outside, it can begin pushing the cycle in the opposite direction.

That's where tourism, business development, migration, manufacturing, entrepreneurship, and digital visibility become important.

The Population Number I Find More Interesting Than the Population Total

There's a population statistic that doesn't get nearly as much attention as the headline number.

Between 2023 and 2024, Lee County's population actually increased by 19 people. Source: USAFacts, using Census Bureau data

That's tiny.

Nobody should look at an increase of 19 people and declare that Lee County is suddenly a booming county.

But look at what caused it.

According to Census Bureau data analyzed by USAFacts, Lee County had positive domestic migration of approximately 183 people between 2023 and 2024. In other words, about 183 more people moved into Lee County from other U.S. counties than moved out. Source: USAFacts, using Census Bureau data

At the same time, natural population change was negative by about 179 people because deaths exceeded births. International migration added approximately 19 people. Source: USAFacts, using Census Bureau data

That's an interesting combination.

Lee County didn't grow because suddenly more babies were being born.

It grew because people were moving here.

And that isn't entirely new.

The same Census-derived data shows positive domestic migration in 2021, 2022, 2023, and 2024 after years of negative domestic migration. Source: USAFacts, using Census Bureau data

Again, I wouldn't call that proof of a turnaround.

Four years of positive domestic migration is more interesting than one year, though.

If that trend continues, it could become one of the most important pieces of Lee County's economic story.

Stabilizing the Population Might Come Before Growing It

There's a tendency to talk about population growth as if the only successful outcome is jumping from 21,000 people to 30,000.

I don't think that's the right way to look at it.

For Lee County, the first victory might simply be stopping the decline.

Imagine two different futures.

In one, Lee County goes:

21,600 → 21,000 → 20,300 → 19,500.

In the other:

21,600 → 21,500 → 21,700 → 22,000 → 22,500.

The second scenario doesn't look spectacular on a national economic-development chart.

Locally, though, it would be a huge difference.

Businesses would know their customer base wasn't disappearing.

Housing demand would be more stable.

People would have more confidence investing.

Young people might have more reasons to stay.

People who left might have more reasons to come back.

And once a community stops losing people, it becomes much easier to talk about actually gaining them.

That's why I think the migration numbers deserve attention.

Lee County Already Has More Entrepreneurs Than You Might Think

Another part of the story is hiding in the business data.

The Census Bureau reports that Lee County had 242 employer establishments and 2,329 employees in 2023. Those establishments accounted for approximately $88.3 million in annual payroll. Source: U.S. Census Bureau

But the county also had 1,062 nonemployer establishments. Source: U.S. Census Bureau

That second number is interesting.

A nonemployer business might be a sole proprietor, independent contractor, freelancer, or another type of business without paid employees. Source: Census Bureau nonemployer definition

In other words, there are more than a thousand small operations in the county that don't necessarily show up in the same way people think about traditional "businesses."

That creates an opportunity.

Economic development doesn't always have to start with convincing a company from another state to build a giant facility.

What if some of the businesses already here simply got bigger?

What if a contractor who currently works alone eventually hires two people?

What if a local retailer opens another location?

What if a home-based business becomes a storefront?

What if a small manufacturer adds another shift?

What if a tourism business turns a side operation into a full-time company?

Those changes don't make national headlines.

But 50 businesses hiring two people each would mean 100 additional jobs.

That's meaningful in a county the size of Lee County.

One of the Best Signs Is That Existing Businesses Are Investing

There's a tendency to measure economic development by how many outside companies a community recruits.

I think there's another measurement that deserves more attention:

Are the businesses already here investing more money?

In 2025, White Rock Truss & Components announced a $1.5 million expansion in Lee County that is expected to create 27 jobs. The investment includes a new lumber shed and equipment upgrades. Source: Virginia Economic Development Partnership

That's not theoretical.

That's an actual company already operating in Lee County deciding to put more money into its operation.

And that's important.

A business doesn't spend $1.5 million because somebody made a nice brochure. Source: Virginia Economic Development Partnership

It spends that money because management believes there is enough demand and enough opportunity to justify expansion.

The project also shows how one local business can connect to other parts of the economy.

White Rock Truss supplies products for the residential and commercial construction industry, so its expansion can support activity beyond the company itself. Source: Virginia Economic Development Partnership

This is why helping existing businesses grow should be treated as seriously as recruiting new ones.

Lee County Has Room for Industrial Development

Tourism isn't the only answer.

And it shouldn't be.

Lee County also has an industrial-development opportunity.

The Lee County Economic Development Authority says Constitutional Oaks Industrial Park contains approximately 220 acres of developable land for industrial and commercial development. Source: Lee County Industrial Development Authority

That's a significant amount of land for a county of roughly 21,600 people. Source: U.S. Census Bureau

Of course, having land doesn't automatically mean companies will come.

A company needs to care about things like:

  • Infrastructure
  • Utilities
  • Transportation
  • Workforce
  • Housing
  • Financing
  • Operating costs
  • Market access
  • Quality of life for employees

But having available land means Lee County has somewhere to put development if the right opportunity comes along.

And Lee County's location shouldn't be overlooked.

The county borders both Kentucky and Tennessee, putting it in a three-state region rather than simply a single-county economy. The Lee County EDA also markets the county's proximity to major interstate corridors as an advantage for business and manufacturing. Source: Lee County Industrial Development Authority

That's something worth selling harder.

A company looking at Lee County isn't necessarily looking at 21,642 potential workers. Source: U.S. Census Bureau

It's looking at the larger region surrounding Lee County.

Tourism Might Be the Biggest Opportunity Sitting in Plain Sight

Now we get to the part that I think deserves much more attention.

Tourism.

When people hear "economic development," they often picture a factory.

Tourism works differently.

A factory brings outside investment and creates jobs.

Tourism brings outside money directly into the community through people who are already traveling.

And Lee County has an enormous tourism asset sitting right next to it.

Cumberland Gap National Historical Park.

Cumberland Gap Is Already Bringing Hundreds of Thousands of People to the Region

In 2024, 729,249 people visited Cumberland Gap National Historical Park. Source: National Park Service

According to the National Park Service, those visitors spent approximately $23.97 million in communities near the park, producing a cumulative local economic benefit of approximately $24.31 million. Source: National Park Service

Read that again.

Nearly three-quarters of a million visitors came to the Cumberland Gap region in one year. Source: National Park Service

And nearly $24 million in visitor spending was associated with that visitation. Source: National Park Service

That doesn't mean Lee County itself received $24 million.

That's an important distinction.

The National Park Service figure covers communities near the park, not Lee County alone.

But that distinction actually makes the question more interesting.

If hundreds of thousands of people are already coming to the area, how much of that spending is Lee County capturing?

That's the question.

The Goal Shouldn't Just Be Getting More Visitors

This is where I think tourism conversations sometimes stop too early.

The goal isn't simply:

"How many people visited?"

The better question is:

"What did those people do while they were here?"

Someone can visit Cumberland Gap and spend almost nothing in Lee County.

They can also:

  • Eat at a local restaurant
  • Fill up their vehicle
  • Buy something from a local shop
  • Book a cabin
  • Stay at a hotel
  • Hire a guide
  • Attend an event
  • Visit another attraction
  • Buy outdoor equipment
  • Come back another weekend

Those are two completely different economic outcomes.

The visitor count is the starting point.

Visitor spending is what turns tourism into economic development.

Lee County Doesn't Have to Invent a Tourism Industry

This is another reason I'm optimistic.

Lee County isn't starting from zero.

The attraction already exists.

The history already exists.

The mountains already exist.

The trails already exist.

The Cumberland Gap already exists.

The question is whether we can build enough around those assets that people decide to stay longer.

A visitor who comes for Cumberland Gap could potentially spend a day exploring the area.

Why couldn't that become a weekend?

Why couldn't the itinerary include a local restaurant?

Why couldn't it include a trail?

Why couldn't it include a local event?

Why couldn't it include a historic site?

Why couldn't it include shopping?

Why couldn't it include a place to stay?

The more reasons you give someone to stay another few hours—or another night—the more opportunity there is for local businesses to benefit.

Virginia's Tourism Industry Is Already Moving in the Right Direction

Lee County isn't trying to create demand for Virginia travel out of thin air.

Virginia's tourism industry is already huge.

State data shows Virginia generated $35.1 billion in visitor spending in 2024, a 5.4% increase from the previous year and 21% above the state's 2019 pre-pandemic level. Virginia also recorded 44.7 million overnight visitors in 2024, a record. Source: Virginia Tourism Corporation

That's a massive tourism market.

The question isn't whether people want to travel to Virginia.

They clearly do.

The question is how much of that travel can be directed toward Southwest Virginia.

And then, within Southwest Virginia, how much can Lee County capture?

That's where marketing starts becoming economic development.

The Region Is Already Generating Tourism Money

Lee County also shouldn't think about tourism as if it exists by itself.

The broader LENOWISCO region has its own tourism economy and collection of attractions.

The region's economic-development planning has identified tourism, outdoor recreation, heritage, trails, and attractions such as Cumberland Gap as important economic assets. Source: LENOWISCO 2024 Comprehensive Economic Development Strategy

That regional approach matters because tourists don't really care about county boundaries.

Someone visiting Southwest Virginia isn't thinking:

"I've crossed the county line, therefore my vacation is over."

They're thinking:

"What's there to do?"

That's an advantage.

A visitor could spend one day around Cumberland Gap, another exploring trails, another visiting another regional attraction, and another eating at local restaurants.

The more connected the region becomes, the more opportunities Lee County has to capture part of that trip.

Outdoor Recreation Could Be a Bigger Business Than We Treat It

There is a reason outdoor recreation keeps showing up in economic-development conversations across Appalachia.

It creates customers for a lot of different businesses.

An ATV rider needs gas.

They need food.

They might need lodging.

They might need repairs.

They might buy equipment.

They might bring friends.

A hiker needs food and lodging.

A family visiting a historic site needs activities and somewhere to eat.

A photographer needs places to explore.

A fisherman needs supplies.

A camper needs groceries.

None of those businesses have to be the attraction itself.

They just have to be positioned to benefit from the people coming for the attraction.

That's where economic development becomes more interesting.

One trail doesn't create one job.

A network of businesses built around the people using that trail can create many jobs.

Housing Might Be More Important Than It Looks

Here's something that doesn't sound as exciting as tourism or a new factory:

housing.

But businesses need workers.

Workers need homes.

And Lee County is already receiving investment in this area.

In September 2025, the Virginia Department of Housing and Community Development announced $5.7 million in funding for housing and wastewater projects in Lee County. Source: Virginia Department of Housing and Community Development

That included $2.5 million for infrastructure supporting 115 housing units associated with Lincoln Memorial University and western Lee County, along with $3.2 million for a wastewater project intended to serve the community and economic development. Source: Virginia Department of Housing and Community Development

That is more than a housing story.

It's an economic-development story.

If a community wants more businesses, it needs people.

If it wants more people, it needs places for them to live.

And if it wants development, it needs infrastructure capable of supporting it.

Those things have to happen together.

The Housing Problem Can Work Both Ways

Imagine Lee County attracts a new employer.

That's good.

But then the company needs 30 workers.

Where do those workers live?

If there aren't enough suitable homes, some may live outside the county.

That means some of the economic benefit leaks out.

On the other hand, if new housing is available and people can move into the county, those workers become:

  • Customers
  • Residents
  • Taxpayers
  • Volunteers
  • Parents
  • Entrepreneurs
  • Community members

That's why housing and business development shouldn't be treated as completely separate issues.

They're connected.

Southwest Virginia Is Still Receiving Economic-Development Investment

Lee County also has something that a lot of small rural communities don't have: a regional economic-development structure specifically designed to help diversify the economy.

The Virginia Coalfield Economic Development Authority reported 27 new projects in 2025, with those projects projected to create 189 full-time and 106 part-time jobs and generate approximately $17.36 million in private investment. VCEDA also reported six expansion projects projected to create or retain 370 full-time and part-time jobs and more than $58 million in private investment. Source: VCEDA 2025 Annual Report

Those numbers are for the broader VCEDA region, not Lee County alone.

That's important.

But they demonstrate that money is still being invested in economic development across Southwest Virginia.

The region isn't being abandoned.

There are still companies expanding.

There are still entrepreneurs starting businesses.

There are still infrastructure projects being funded.

There is still an effort to diversify the economy.

Lee County has to make sure it is positioned to capture its share of that activity.

Maybe the Biggest Opportunity Is Outside Money

There's one idea I think is worth focusing on.

Lee County doesn't necessarily need to get everyone to move here to become economically stronger.

It needs more money coming into the county.

Think about a tourist.

They don't live here.

But they spend $200 here.

Think about a remote worker.

They might live here while earning a salary from a company somewhere else.

Think about an online business.

The owner lives here while selling to customers across the country.

Think about a manufacturer.

The workers live here while the company sells products outside the region.

All four examples bring money into Lee County from somewhere else.

That's powerful.

Because a community of 21,600 people can support more economic activity if it isn't relying exclusively on the spending power of those 21,600 people. Source: U.S. Census Bureau

The Internet Changes What a Rural Economy Can Do

This is where the modern economy gives Lee County an opportunity previous generations didn't have.

Geography still matters.

A lot.

But it doesn't matter quite as much for certain types of businesses.

A web designer can work with someone in California.

A software developer can work for a company in New York.

An online store can sell nationwide.

A consultant can serve clients remotely.

A photographer can sell services to visitors.

A local manufacturer can sell products outside Southwest Virginia.

A tourism business can attract someone hundreds of miles away.

The customer doesn't always have to be local anymore.

That's a big deal for a rural county.

Digital Visibility Is Becoming Part of Economic Development

This is also where I think Lee County has a relatively inexpensive opportunity.

If someone searches:

"Things to do in Lee County Virginia"

what do they find?

If someone searches:

"Restaurants near Cumberland Gap"

how many Lee County businesses appear?

If someone searches:

"Cabins near Cumberland Gap"

how easy is it to find local lodging?

If someone searches:

"Industrial land Southwest Virginia"

does Lee County immediately show up?

If someone searches:

"Start a business in Southwest Virginia"

can they easily find information about Lee County?

Those aren't just marketing questions.

They're economic-development questions.

Because if people can't find something, they can't buy it.

And if businesses can't be found, customers go somewhere else.

Search Results Are the New Highway Sign

For decades, communities invested in signs telling people where to go.

Today, Google does something similar.

Someone planning a trip doesn't necessarily see a billboard first.

They might type a question into their phone.

They might search Google Maps.

They might look at reviews.

They might check a website.

They might look at photos.

They might search Instagram or Facebook.

That means a town or county's online presence can influence what happens in the physical economy.

A visitor may search for a restaurant and choose the first one that looks trustworthy.

A family may search for a place to stay and book the one with the best website.

An entrepreneur may research communities before deciding where to move.

A company may research available industrial property before making a phone call.

Being discoverable matters.

This Doesn't Mean SEO Can Fix an Economy

It can't.

A website isn't going to solve population decline.

SEO isn't going to build a factory.

Facebook isn't going to install a sewer line.

Digital marketing isn't going to replace workforce development.

But that's not the point.

The point is that Lee County already has physical assets.

Digital visibility can help those assets reach more people.

Think about it like this:

A tourism attraction is the product.

The road gets people there.

The internet can convince them to come.

The business provides the experience.

The visitor spends the money.

That's the relationship.

Lee County Has an Identity Other Places Can't Easily Copy

One of the things I think Lee County should stop apologizing for is being rural.

The county doesn't need to become a smaller version of a city.

It has something different.

It has the Appalachian mountains.

It has Cumberland Gap.

It has a border with Kentucky and Tennessee.

It has a long history.

It has outdoor recreation.

It has small communities.

It has land.

It has housing costs that are lower than many larger markets. Source: U.S. Census Bureau housing data

It has the kind of scenery that other communities work hard to feature in marketing campaigns.

You can't manufacture a mountain.

You can't build another Cumberland Gap.

You can't recreate Appalachian history.

Those things already exist.

The job is figuring out how to turn them into economic advantages without destroying what makes them valuable in the first place.

The Goal Shouldn't Be to Turn Lee County Into Knoxville

This is where I think economic-development conversations can go wrong.

Lee County doesn't need to become a major city.

It doesn't need skyscrapers.

It doesn't need thousands of tourists packed into every street.

It doesn't need to sacrifice its identity to say it is "growing."

A successful future could look much simpler.

More people working.

More businesses staying open.

More young people finding opportunities.

More tourists spending money locally.

More houses being built.

More entrepreneurs starting companies.

More manufacturers expanding.

More people moving in than moving out.

More money circulating locally.

That's growth.

It just looks different in a county of 21,600 people. Source: U.S. Census Bureau

What Would a Healthy Lee County Economy Actually Look Like?

I'd argue that Lee County shouldn't judge its future by population alone.

A healthy economy would probably show several things happening at the same time.

Population would stabilize.

Domestic migration would remain positive.

Businesses would increase.

Existing businesses would expand.

Wages would rise.

Tourism spending would increase.

Visitors would stay longer.

More housing would become available.

More entrepreneurs would become employers.

Downtown businesses would become viable.

And more outside money would enter the county.

If those things happen together, population growth may eventually follow.

But it doesn't necessarily have to happen first.

There Are Really Three Possible Futures

The first is continued decline.

Lee County continues losing residents.

Young people continue leaving.

Businesses struggle to find customers.

Tourists pass through without spending much locally.

Investment happens occasionally but doesn't build momentum.

Under that scenario, the county continues trying to manage decline.

That's the risk if nothing changes.

The second is stabilization.

The population stops falling as quickly.

Domestic migration stays positive or close to neutral.

Tourism continues growing.

Businesses expand slowly.

Housing improves.

Infrastructure improves.

More entrepreneurs start businesses.

Lee County doesn't suddenly become a boomtown.

But it becomes more stable.

Honestly, that would be a major accomplishment.

The third is sustainable growth.

This is the scenario worth chasing.

Tourists increase.

More visitors stay overnight.

Local businesses capture more of their spending.

Businesses expand.

New businesses open.

Jobs increase.

Housing follows.

People move into the county.

Population stabilizes.

The larger customer base supports more businesses.

More businesses create more jobs.

More jobs attract more residents.

Eventually, the cycle starts feeding itself.

That's how I think Lee County could realistically grow.

Not through one giant miracle.

Through a lot of smaller successes that start reinforcing each other.

What Could Lee County Do Right Now?

If I were looking at this strictly from an economic-development perspective, I'd focus on a handful of things.

Make tourism easier to discover.

If someone wants to visit Lee County, make it incredibly easy for them to find everything they can do while they're here.

Help existing businesses grow.

Don't only ask, "What company can we recruit?"

Ask, "What can we do to help the companies already here hire their first employee, open their second location, or reach customers outside the county?"

Build around outdoor recreation.

Trails and natural resources aren't just recreational amenities.

They're potential customers for restaurants, lodging, retail, guides, repair shops, outfitters, and events.

Keep investing in housing and infrastructure.

Businesses need workers, and workers need places to live.

Market industrial property aggressively.

If land is available, companies need to know about it.

Build a better digital presence.

Make businesses, attractions, events, lodging, available property, and investment opportunities easy to find online.

Work regionally.

A tourist doesn't care where Lee County ends and another county begins.

Neither does an employer.

Track the numbers.

Population.

Migration.

Jobs.

Wages.

Tourism spending.

Hotel occupancy.

Business creation.

Business closures.

Investment.

Housing.

If the goal is growth, the community should be able to see whether it is actually happening.

A Renewed Chamber Could Help Connect the Pieces

One encouraging local effort is already trying to create more of that connection.

Heather Hitchler is working to rekindle a chamber of commerce for Lee County—a place where local businesses can share information, coordinate events, advocate for their needs, and build momentum together.

According to Hitchler, more than 50 businesses have already expressed interest in the effort. Source: Heather Hitchler on Facebook

That matters because many of the opportunities discussed in this article depend on businesses working together.

A visitor is more likely to stay longer when restaurants, shops, lodging, attractions, and events are easy to discover as part of one connected experience.

An entrepreneur is more likely to keep going when there is a network of other owners to learn from.

A county is more likely to tell a compelling economic story when its businesses have a shared voice.

Rebuilding a chamber will not solve every challenge Lee County faces, but interest from more than 50 businesses is a meaningful sign that local owners want greater coordination—and are willing to help create it. Source: Heather Hitchler on Facebook

That kind of grassroots organization could become one more piece of the momentum Lee County needs.

And rural economic development is difficult.

But I also don't think the evidence supports saying Lee County is doomed.

There are too many pieces moving in the other direction.

Those aren't predictions.

They're things that are already happening.

The Bigger Question Is What We Do With Them

Lee County Has Something Many Places Are Trying to Buy

There are communities all over the country spending enormous amounts of money trying to manufacture an identity.

They build downtown districts.

They create tourism campaigns.

They develop trails.

They advertise outdoor recreation.

Lee County already has many of those things.

The problem isn't necessarily a lack of assets.

It's often a lack of visibility, coordination, investment, and scale.

The Opportunity Isn't to Pretend Lee County Is Already Successful

I think there's actually something powerful about being honest about the situation.

Lee County has problems.

Population decline is real.

Poverty is real.

Businesses struggle.

Some buildings sit empty.

Pretending those things aren't happening doesn't help.

A community can acknowledge its problems while still believing its future can be different.

In fact, I think that's where good economic development starts.

The Next Ten Years Could Be More Important Than the Last Ten

Lee County probably isn't going to transform overnight.

That's not realistic.

But 10 years is a long time.

Imagine what happens if over the next decade:

Together, they could change the direction of the county.

And direction matters.

Lee County Doesn't Need a Miracle. It Needs Momentum.

Lee County doesn't need one company to magically create 2,000 jobs.

It needs enough things going right at the same time that people start believing something different is happening.

A business owner sees another business succeed.

The Most Important Asset Might Be the People Who Are Already Here

It's easy to talk about attracting outsiders.

But Lee County already has people who care enough to stay.

It has business owners who keep opening their doors.

It has entrepreneurs trying new things.

It has people working in manufacturing.

It has teachers, healthcare workers, tradespeople, farmers, contractors, artists, service workers, students, retirees, volunteers, and community organizations.

Economic development shouldn't be about replacing those people.

It should be about giving them more opportunities.

If existing residents can make more money, start more businesses, hire more people, and sell more products outside the county, the entire community benefits.

And That's Why the Digital Side Matters

A local business can have the best product in the county and still lose a customer because somebody couldn't find it online.

A tourism attraction can be incredible and still get overlooked because nobody knows what else is nearby.

A county can have industrial land and still struggle to attract investors if the information is difficult to find.

A community can have affordable housing and beautiful scenery and still miss potential new residents if its online presence doesn't tell that story.

None of these problems require a new interstate.

Sometimes they require better information.

Better websites.

Better search visibility.

Better maps.

Better business listings.

Better content.

Better photography.

Better event information.

Better connections between businesses and attractions.

That's a relatively small investment compared with building physical infrastructure.

And it can reach people far beyond the county.

The Road Ahead

So where does that leave Lee County?

I think the honest answer is:

Cautiously optimistic.

Not because the problems have disappeared.

They haven't.

And not because one statistic proves that a turnaround is happening.

It doesn't.

I'm optimistic because Lee County still has something to work with.

The population decline has slowed enough in recent years to make migration worth watching. Source: USAFacts, using Census Bureau data

Tourism is bringing hundreds of thousands of people into the region. Source: National Park Service

Cumberland Gap is an enormous existing asset.

Virginia's tourism economy is growing. Source: Virginia Tourism Corporation

Businesses are investing. Source: Virginia Economic Development Partnership

Industrial land is available. Source: Lee County Industrial Development Authority

Housing and wastewater infrastructure are receiving major investment. Source: Virginia Department of Housing and Community Development

Regional economic-development organizations are still putting money into Southwest Virginia. Source: VCEDA 2025 Annual Report

And there is already a large base of small businesses and entrepreneurs here. Source: U.S. Census Bureau

The pieces exist.

The question is whether they get connected.

Lee County's Future Isn't Decided Yet

If we only look at the population chart, it is easy to conclude that Lee County's best days are behind it.

I don't think the data gives us enough reason to say that.

It gives us a reason to be realistic.

But it also gives us a reason to pay attention.

Because there are signs of something worth investigating.

People are moving here. Source: USAFacts, using Census Bureau data

Visitors are coming here. Source: National Park Service

Businesses are investing here. Source: Virginia Economic Development Partnership

Infrastructure is being built here. Source: Virginia Department of Housing and Community Development

Land is available here. Source: Lee County Industrial Development Authority

Entrepreneurs are working here. Source: U.S. Census Bureau

The question is whether those individual things can become a larger economic story.

Maybe Lee County doesn't need to become a boomtown.

Maybe the better goal is something more achievable:

A county where businesses have a reason to stay.

A county where entrepreneurs have a reason to start.

A county where visitors have a reason to spend the night.

A county where young people have a reason to stay.

A county where people who left have a reason to come back.

A county where outside money keeps finding its way into local businesses.

And eventually, if enough of those things happen together, population growth may become the result rather than the starting point.

Lee County's story isn't finished.

In fact, I think we're still early enough in the next chapter that the direction is going to depend heavily on what happens now.

The county already has the mountains.

It already has the history.

It already has Cumberland Gap.

It already has businesses.

It already has entrepreneurs.

It already has land.

It already has people who care about the community.

What it needs is to connect those pieces, tell the story better, and make it easier for the rest of the world to discover what is already here.

Lee County doesn't have to become something it isn't.

It just needs to become a stronger version of what it already is.

And if that happens, the economic future of Lee County could look very different from its past.
